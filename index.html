Polytopes and mixed extreme points of finite Fourier Kirkwood–Dirac state spaces

Finite Fourier KD positivity · Paper 1 · hys-ops

For a finite abelian group, the position and Fourier bases give a Kirkwood–Dirac (KD) array for each quantum state. This work asks when the states with real, nonnegative KD arrays can be generated by a finite list of states through probabilistic mixing.

What the Kirkwood–Dirac distribution measures

For position basis x and Fourier basis χ in finite dimension, the KD entry of a state ρ is Qρ(x,χ) = ⟨χ|x⟩⟨x|ρ|χ⟩. KD positivity requires these entries to be real and nonnegative.

The result

The Fourier KD-positive state space is a polytope exactly for the trivial group and for cyclic groups of prime-power order. In those cases it is the convex hull of its pure KD-positive states. For every other finite abelian group it has exactly continuum-many mixed extreme points, including an embedded copy of the closed unit interval. Adding finitely many mixed states cannot generate the whole space.

For two qubits, with the position and Fourier bases of the group C₂ × C₂, an explicit rational curve gives distinct rank-three exposed points. Each is the unique minimizer of a suitable linear functional on the KD-positive state space. The curve also lifts to every finite abelian group containing C₂ × C₂, with rank and exposure preserved.

Relation to prior work: The Bruneton-led note (23 November 2026 revision, Theorem 1) gives the pure-state hull classification for all finite abelian groups. Both proofs reduce to two prime-order factors and use a Bell-type test. The geometric results here go further: they ask whether any finite set of states can generate the full body and describe the mixed extreme points.

Same dimension, different geometry

C₄ and C₂ × C₂ both use a four-dimensional Hilbert space, but their Fourier pairs differ. The cyclic Fourier pair gives a polytope; the two-qubit Walsh pair gives an explicit continuous family of mixed exposed states.

How the proof works

In the cases outside the classification, a subgroup generated by two prime-order directions supplies a pair of position and Fourier reflections in each factor. Their product construction gives a singular positive semidefinite matrix whose KD entries are all strictly positive. At that point the boundary comes from matrix positivity rather than a vanishing KD entry. The proof uses perturbations of this boundary matrix to show that a finite vertex list would force a common eigenline for the position and Fourier projectors. Such a line cannot exist.

For the two-qubit example, six zero KD entries and a prescribed kernel vector determine the matrix up to its trace. Those constraints also produce the linear functional that exposes each point on the curve.

Where the idea came from

In the earlier SISA work, a single address was used to handle several possible reading orders at once. That suggested trying one test against all pure KD-positive states, rather than comparing them individually. The finite KD argument turns that analogy into a separating functional. Section 5 gives one construction for all prime pairs, including equal primes, where the test also accounts for oblique directions. The polytope result then uses the reflection construction in a different way: it studies the boundary of the positive semidefinite cone.

Reading one result? The two-qubit example can be read on its own, without first reading the general classification.

How the proofs developed

The pure-hull question supplied the starting point. The strict singular state leads to nonpolyhedrality and then to the general mixed interval. The two-qubit curve is an explicit branch, not a premise for all finite groups.
